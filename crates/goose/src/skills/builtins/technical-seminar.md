# Technical seminar and project review

## Budget the time first

A slide takes about a minute when spoken properly. For a fifteen minute
seminar that is twelve to fourteen content slides, not thirty. Overrunning
is penalised more than finishing a minute early.

Reserve the last three minutes for questions, and rehearse aloud with a
timer at least twice. Reading silently badly underestimates the time.

## Slide structure for a technical seminar

1. Title, presenter, guide, department, date
2. Outline, one line per section
3. Introduction and motivation: why this matters
4. Problem statement, stated as one precise sentence
5. Literature survey: three to five prior works and what each left open
6. Proposed methodology, with a block diagram
7. Implementation details
8. Results, as graphs or tables, compared against a baseline
9. Discussion, including limitations
10. Conclusion and future scope
11. References
12. Thank you and questions

## Slide design

Six lines per slide, six words per line, as an upper bound. Slides support
the talk; they are not the report. Font no smaller than 24 point, because
the back row cannot read 14 point.

Every graph needs axis labels with units, a legend, and a caption. A result
without a baseline to compare against means nothing to an examiner.

Cite any figure taken from a paper on the slide where it appears.

## The questions examiners actually ask

Prepare answers to these before the day:

- Why did you choose this approach over the alternatives?
- What is the limitation of your work?
- How did you validate the result, and against what?
- What is novel here compared with the papers you surveyed?
- Which part did you implement yourself?
- What would you do with six more months?

"I do not know, but I would find out by ..." is a better answer than a
confident guess. Examiners probe guesses.

## Delivery

Speak to the audience, not the screen. Do not read the slides aloud; the
audience reads faster than you speak. Pause at each section boundary.

## How to help

Produce the slide-by-slide outline with speaker notes and a time budget per
slide. Flag any slide carrying too much text. Generate the likely question
list from the specific content, not a generic list, and note where the
student's material is thin enough to attract a hard question.
